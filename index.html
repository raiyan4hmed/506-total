1 The logic of a language test

Imagine a higher secondary school in Bangladesh where students complete two years of English classes. The teacher wants to decide whether graduates can understand a short spoken announcement, discuss a familiar problem, read an informational passage, and write a coherent request. These are abilities we cannot inspect directly. We arrange tasks, observe responses, assign scores and make a decision. Each step can go wrong.

For example, if we claim to assess speaking but ask only grammar multiple choice questions, the task gives weak evidence of spoken interaction. If we actually interview students but score only grammatical accuracy, the scoring ignores important parts of speaking. If the room microphone fails, a capable student may seem unable to listen. If we use the resulting score to decide university admission although the tasks were designed only for classroom diagnosis, the score has been used for a new and unsupported purpose.

Working chain: intended ability and decision → representative task → performance under fair conditions → appropriate scoring → defensible interpretation → intended consequence. Testing samples performance; it never observes a person's entire competence. The size and quality of the sample determine how much confidence we can place in the inference.

Assessment is the broader process of gathering and interpreting evidence about learning. A test is a more structured instrument or event within assessment. Evaluation uses evidence to judge the quality or effectiveness of a learner, course, test or programme against some purpose or criteria. Thus a speaking test yields evidence; a teacher uses that evidence alongside classroom observation to assess learning; a department evaluates whether the course has met its goals. The labels overlap in practice, so always identify the decision being made.

Hughes and Hughes (2020, chs. 1–2) present testing as practical problem solving: a test needs to serve its purpose while balancing validity, reliability, beneficial effects on teaching and feasibility. Bachman and Palmer (1996) similarly frame test usefulness in relation to the particular situation of use. Neither framework means that a single number, such as the highest reliability coefficient, automatically makes a test best.

2 Why we test and how tests differ

Purpose first

The same learner may take four tests in the same week for four distinct decisions. Suppose Amina arrives at an English programme. To decide which class she should join, the institution uses a placement test. Her teacher then investigates why she struggles with listening for detail: that is diagnostic assessment. At the end of a taught unit, the teacher checks attainment of its learning objectives: an achievement test. A university later asks whether her English suffices for English medium study, regardless of where she learned it: a proficiency test. A test can serve more than one purpose, but it needs evidence for each intended use.

PurposeQuestion and suitable resultConcrete example
ProficiencyCan the person use English to the required level beyond a particular course?An admissions assessment of academic English.
AchievementHas the person met course objectives? Progress tests occur during the course; final tests at its end.A course test of taught reading and writing objectives.
DiagnosticWhich subskills or patterns of error need attention? It should return an informative profile.Identify weak inference reading and listening for specific information.
PlacementWhich class or level best fits the student now?Assign students to intermediate or advanced groups.

An important distinction in Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 3) is that achievement can be constructed around what a textbook covered or around what learners were meant to achieve. An objectives based test is stronger when textbook coverage and intended learning diverge. A diagnostic test differs from placement because a level decision alone does not explain a student's errors.

Other classifications answer different questions

Direct versus indirect: To assess writing directly, have students write for a real audience; to assess speaking directly, elicit speech. Grammar recognition items may indicate knowledge associated with writing, but are an indirect measure of writing. Reading and listening need observable responses to demonstrate comprehension. Direct tasks must still be sampled and scored well: a single narrow speech prompt cannot represent all interaction.

Discrete point versus integrative: A gap requiring one verb form targets a separate feature. Summarising a lecture integrates listening, selection of ideas, vocabulary and writing. Integration does not by itself guarantee an authentic communicative purpose: a dictation also draws on several abilities.

Objective versus subjective scoring: These terms describe how much judgment the scorer exercises. A multiple choice answer key allows objective scoring; a spoken explanation needs human judgment. Subjective scoring can be made reasonably consistent using descriptors, training, double marking and moderation. An objectively scored item can still measure the wrong ability.

Norm referenced versus criterion referenced: A norm interpretation compares Amina with a reference group: she is above the 80th percentile, for example. A criterion interpretation asks whether she can meet a defined standard, such as explaining and supporting an opinion in a three minute discussion. A percentile is not a percentage of items correct; a 75th percentile indicates performance at or above roughly 75% of the reference group. A cut score must specify a defensible performance standard; merely saying “pass at 50” does not explain what successful students can do.

Paper, computer and adaptive: These describe delivery. A computer based test can show identical items to all candidates. An adaptive test estimates a candidate's level and chooses subsequent items accordingly; it requires an adequately calibrated item bank and sound rules. Delivery mode alone says nothing about whether the test is valid.

Check the classification

“Write a letter to a school principal as part of the final course examination” is simultaneously an achievement test, a direct writing task, an integrative task, and probably subjectively scored. These are compatible descriptions because they answer different questions.

3 The quality of a test as an argument

Return to the school. Its English test is used to certify readiness to communicate in ordinary academic situations. A defensible claim is: students who pass can handle the specified situations. What evidence would justify this claim? We need tasks that represent the target abilities, performances that remain reasonably consistent, administration within the school's resources, and consequences that do not undermine learning. These are the connected roles of validity, reliability, practicality and backwash.

Validity means a warranted interpretation for a specified use

In a familiar formulation, Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 4) say a test is valid when it measures accurately what it intends to measure. The more precise modern question is whether the evidence supports the interpretation and use of scores for a particular purpose. An excellent classroom grammar quiz can be valid for checking article knowledge and invalid as the sole evidence that someone can deliver an oral presentation.

Construct: the ability or attribute we mean to assess. If “speaking ability” includes comprehensibility, fluency, vocabulary, grammar and interaction, a test of memorised speeches underrepresents interaction. If a speaking prompt requires obscure geographical knowledge, scores may reflect irrelevant subject knowledge. These are construct underrepresentation and construct irrelevant influence. A clear construct definition controls task selection and scoring.

Content evidence: Do the tasks represent the skills, content, text types, difficulty and situations in the specifications? If a course teaches reading, writing, listening and speaking, but a written final uses only grammar editing, large areas are missing. Compare a test blueprint with the actual tasks; ask knowledgeable reviewers to check coverage. Sampling matters: one writing prompt about cricket may disadvantage strong writers unfamiliar with the topic.

Criterion related evidence: Do scores relate appropriately to an independent, credible measure? Concurrent evidence compares measures taken near the same time, for example a short oral screen against a careful extended oral assessment. Predictive evidence asks whether a score forecasts later performance, for example success in an English medium course. A correlation by itself does not prove every proposed use or establish cause.

Face validity: Does the test appear relevant and credible to learners and teachers? An oral test that contains no spoken response may be rejected as implausible. Hughes and Hughes distinguish this appearance from stronger technical evidence: a test can look sensible yet assess too narrow a construct.

Scoring validity: Do the scoring criteria reflect the intended ability? An essay awarded mainly for handwriting measures penmanship alongside writing. A reading comprehension task whose answers require long polished essays may also measure writing skill. The task, scoring and interpretation must all fit.

Worked judgment

A school says its English examination measures “communicative ability” but awards 70% for grammar manipulation and 30% for memorised paragraphs. The strongest criticism is not simply “the test is bad.” It is that the evidence is insufficient for the claimed communicative inference: listening and spontaneous speaking are absent, writing is artificially constrained, and relevant abilities are underrepresented. A realistic improvement is a blueprint with sampled listening and speaking tasks, a direct writing task, suitable reading texts and aligned rubrics.

Reliability means consistency of scores under comparable conditions

Imagine Amina is rated 8/10 by one examiner and 4/10 by another for the same performance. Or a poor loudspeaker reduces her listening score on one day. Such scores are unstable. Reliability concerns the consistency or dependability of measurement. If a result changes because of scorer, room, unclear instructions, too few tasks or chance choice of topics rather than the intended ability, the error is too large.

Test–retest reliability compares scores from the same test administered twice when ability should be stable; practice effects and real learning can complicate interpretation. Parallel forms reliability compares equivalent forms. Internal consistency examines how coherently items contribute to a score, though a deliberately broad test need not have every part behave identically. Inter rater reliability concerns agreement among judges; intra rater reliability concerns a judge's consistency over time. A reliability coefficient is typically reported between 0 and 1 for relevant methods, with values nearer 1 indicating greater consistency; its adequacy depends on stakes and purpose. A coefficient is an estimate for scores in a population and context, not a permanent property stamped onto a test.

Improve reliability by writing unambiguous instructions, including enough appropriate independent tasks, standardising timing and audio, preparing answer keys, using clear analytic rubrics, training and monitoring raters, and double marking consequential performances. Consistency is necessary for a sound interpretation, but consistent measurement of the wrong thing remains invalid.

Practicality and backwash complete the design problem

Practicality concerns whether a test can be developed, administered, marked and reported with available time, staff, funds, rooms and equipment. An hour long individual interview for 20,000 candidates is likely impracticable; a short paired task with trained raters may be feasible. Yet convenience cannot justify deleting the ability the decision actually requires. Sampling a few representative tasks is an informed compromise.

Backwash (also called washback) is the influence of testing on teaching and learning. If a speaking objective is assessed through memorised written answers, instruction may narrow into memorisation: harmful backwash. If speaking is assessed through purposeful interaction and students practise interaction, the effect may be beneficial. Backwash is an empirical effect, not an automatic guarantee: teachers, textbooks, stakes and resources all mediate it. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 6) recommend testing valued abilities directly, publishing specifications, using broad representative samples, sharing criteria, and giving timely feedback. Teaching should establish worthy learning goals; the test should reflect them, then evidence from assessment should help improve teaching.

Relationship: Higher reliability through objective grammar MCQs may make marking easier but can lower validity for a speaking decision and encourage narrow preparation. A direct oral task can improve validity and backwash while requiring more rater training to protect reliability and more resources to remain practical. The question is how to design a workable balance for this purpose, not which single property wins.

How to discuss the current HSC examination responsibly

Do not claim precise features of the current HSC papers from an unprovided or potentially changed version. In an exam, frame a conditional, evidence based analysis: if the operative English examination samples mainly written grammar, reading and composition while the stated curriculum expects listening and oral communication, its scores cannot fully support claims about the latter abilities. Standardised instructions and detailed keys could improve consistency; essay and paragraph marks need common rubrics and moderation. Large cohort size constrains practicality, so phased internal listening and speaking assessment with teacher support may be an implementation option. Verify the year's actual papers and curriculum before making an unqualified claim.

4 From a purpose to a working test

A teacher says, “I need a 40 minute end of term test.” Forty minutes is a resource constraint, not a test specification. We first ask which decision the scores support, who the learners are, which abilities and situations matter, and what evidence would be sufficient. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 7) discuss an iterative development process, while Fulcher and Davidson (2007) give particular attention to specifications and the chain from a construct to tasks.

Write a specification before writing questions

A test specification is a recipe detailed enough that different writers can build comparable versions. It states the purpose, target population, construct, tasks and content, text features, number of items and prompts, timing, instructions, response mode, scoring and reporting rules, and administration conditions. An accompanying blueprint maps objectives to tasks and marks. The specification guards against a convenient but unrepresentative set of questions.

Miniature blueprint for a 40 mark school test
Target abilityEvidence and conditionsMarks
Read for gist and detailOne 350 word age appropriate passage; gist item plus four short answers; 15 minutes.10

Interact orallyThree minute paired decision task, scheduled separately; two trained raters where feasible.10

The precise allocation is illustrative. If the real decision concerns academic reading alone, the same distribution would be inappropriate. Every mark and minute should follow the purpose.

Then draft multiple prompts, write keys and scoring descriptors in advance, obtain expert review for construct coverage, language level, bias and clarity, and pilot with comparable learners. Analyze responses, interview students about confusing items, revise tasks and rubrics, and check timing. Standardise administration, prepare a marking guide, train scorers, score the operational test, report interpretable results, and review consequences after use. Pilot testing is a trial for revision; it is not merely asking whether a sample gets a high average.

Four common task formats and what they buy

Multiple choice: an item has a stem and alternatives, including a key and plausible distractors. It samples many points and is fast to score. Write a clear question, one defensible key, plausible distractors based on likely errors, and avoid clues such as a markedly longer correct option. Guessing and poor distractors weaken evidence. A reading MCQ can test inference if it truly requires reading; a grammar MCQ cannot substitute for production.

Short answer: students produce a word, phrase or brief explanation. It reduces guessing, but acceptable variants must be anticipated in the key. In listening, allow equivalent wording and decide whether spelling matters to the intended construct before marking.

Essay or extended writing: it elicits organisation, cohesion, argument and register, but takes time and requires rubrics. Provide a purpose, audience, length, time and realistic prompt; sample more than one genre if the claim is broad.

Performance task: students use language to complete an action such as resolving a problem in pairs or summarising a notice for a visitor. It can give strong direct evidence; maintain standard conditions and assess the ability actually elicited. A group role play may obscure an individual's contribution unless the procedure addresses this.

Administration is part of measurement

Equal printed copies, audible audio, stable timing, comparable prompts and secure procedures are conditions for interpreting differences between students as differences in ability. If one class hears the recording clearly and another does not, the resulting score gap reflects administration. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 18) place administration inside responsible test practice for this reason.

5 Assessing reading listening writing and speaking

For each skill, begin with the claim you wish to make, elicit the relevant behaviour, and avoid scoring irrelevant difficulties. Hughes and Hughes (2020, chs. 9–12) organise practical skill testing around task choice and scoring. The distinctions below are useful in both design and evaluation questions.

Reading

Reading involves constructing meaning from a written text for a purpose. A reader may skim for a main idea, scan for a detail, infer an unstated implication, identify a writer's stance, or integrate information across paragraphs. A sound test samples the reading operations the course or target situation requires. If the target is academic reading, include suitable expository texts; if it is everyday survival reading, notices and forms may matter more.

Choose texts with appropriate length, readability, topic familiarity and cultural accessibility. Then ask questions that can be answered from the text, not from general knowledge alone. A main idea question must concern the whole passage; a local detail question must have a traceable answer; an inference must be defensible from textual evidence. Short answers and MCQs are possible. Avoid copying the exact sentence that reveals every answer: students may match words without comprehension. Avoid making the response writing so demanding that writing ability dominates a reading score. A vocabulary in context item must require meaning in that context rather than an isolated dictionary definition.

A valid reading choice

For a 350 word article about safe drinking water, ask students to identify the principal recommendation, locate one specific condition, and infer why a suggested practice is important. The items sample different operations. If most answers can be obtained from the title or general knowledge, revise them.

Listening

Listening requires making sense of spoken language in real time: identifying gist, locating specific information, following a sequence, understanding attitude, or responding in an interaction. A written transcript followed by questions is a reading test, not a listening test. Listening passages should match the purpose: a simple school announcement for school information; a short lecture extract for academic note taking.

State who is speaking, the task purpose, the permitted preview time, number of playings, audio length, pace and response format. Use comprehensible, consistent recording and test it in the actual room. A dialogue with too many speakers or unfamiliar proper names can introduce accidental difficulty. A one word answer may capture comprehension without demanding advanced writing. If spelling is not a target, accept intelligible phonetic approximations where the meaning is unmistakable. Give adequate reading time for questions, otherwise fast reading becomes a hidden listening prerequisite. The number of playings should reflect the intended situation and level, rather than a universal rule.

Writing

Writing ability means creating a text that serves a purpose for a reader. Consider task fulfilment, relevant ideas, organisation, cohesion, lexical resources, grammatical control and appropriate register. A polished copy of a memorised model cannot show flexible writing. A grammar editing exercise provides evidence of editing or grammatical knowledge, not sufficient evidence of composition.

Available techniques: sentence completion, guided paragraph, picture or data description, email/letter, summary, argumentative response and extended essay. They differ in the range of writing elicited. For a first year English Honours group, a purposeful 250–300 word argument or comparison, with a clear audience and two defensible positions, can elicit organisation and argument. For large Bangladeshi classes with limited marking time, two shorter purposeful tasks may sample more broadly than one long essay, while a concise analytic rubric and moderation keep marking manageable. Controlled tasks help diagnose specific language features but should not be the sole evidence of authentic writing ability.

Writing task that can actually be marked

“Your department is considering a weekly student led reading circle. Write a 220–280 word email to the department chair recommending whether to introduce it. Give two reasons, address one likely concern, and propose a practical arrangement. You have 35 minutes.” The audience, genre, purpose, content obligations, length and time are explicit. A rubric can rate response to task, organisation, language and audience awareness. Avoid requiring obscure literary facts if writing, rather than content recall, is the construct.

Speaking

Speaking includes conveying meaning intelligibly, organising turns, responding to others, speaking with sufficient fluency and using appropriate grammar and vocabulary. Asking students to recite a prepared paragraph samples memory and pronunciation but gives limited evidence about interaction. Use more than one task where feasible: short warm up, individual description or explanation, and pair information gap or problem solving. A conversation with a partner elicits interaction but partner behaviour can influence scores; standard prompts, examiner scripts and individual scoring reduce this problem.

For primary school children, use brief, familiar, age appropriate tasks with pictures or everyday objects: identifying an item, describing what a person is doing, asking a classmate for missing information. Instructions should be simple; a friendly warm up lowers avoidable anxiety. Rate communicative success and intelligibility before minor grammatical errors, according to explicit grade appropriate descriptors. At higher secondary level, add opinion, explanation and negotiation tasks. Examiner speech, timing, task difficulty and rating criteria should be comparable across students.

Worked combined listening and speaking test

Purpose: assess whether higher secondary learners can understand everyday academic information and exchange reasons for a choice. Listening, 10 marks: play a 90 second recording of two students discussing a campus club schedule twice; ask two gist and three detail questions, two marks each, permitting brief equivalent answers. Trial the recording and key with comparable learners. Speaking, 10 marks: give each pair a simple timetable with two overlapping activities and ask them to choose one event to attend, explaining and responding to each other for four minutes. Rate each student individually: task fulfilment and interaction 4, intelligibility and fluency 3, language range and control 3. Use common prompts, a sample performance, rater training and moderation. For practicality, test in pairs on a scheduled rota and record a sample for review, subject to consent and local rules.

Validity check: listeners must hear the recording, speakers must negotiate a choice, and the rubric must reward comprehension and interaction. Reliability check: common audio, comparable prompts, scoring exemplars and trained raters. Practicality check: portable equipment, realistic pair schedule and a short rubric. Fairness check: topic familiarity, accessible audio, clearly explained procedure and justified accommodations.

6 Scoring performance and interpreting results

A test may elicit excellent evidence and still fail if marking distorts it. A rubric tells raters what dimensions to judge and what different quality levels look like. A criterion names a feature, such as organisation; a descriptor says what performance at a level demonstrates. “Good grammar” is too vague. “Uses a range of simple and some complex sentences; errors seldom obscure meaning” is more usable.

Holistic and analytic scoring

Holistic scoring assigns one overall score for the whole performance after considering all relevant features. It can be relatively quick and matches an overall communication judgment, but provides little diagnostic detail and can hide a weakness behind a strength. Analytic scoring rates named dimensions separately and combines them, for example task fulfilment 0–4, organisation 0–3, language 0–3. It gives more diagnostic feedback and makes attention to criteria visible, but takes more time and dimensions can overlap. Neither approach automatically ensures validity or reliability; the choice depends on purpose and training.

Usable short analytic writing rubric out of 10

Before operational marking, add level descriptors and sample scripts for each band. Decide how to handle incomplete or off topic responses. Have two markers independently score a sample; discuss disagreements before continuing.

Objective and subjective: The correct answer to a well specified MCQ can be scored without a judge's discretion. A written argument requires judgment. “Objective” concerns scoring, not fairness, quality or test purpose. Double marking, blind marking where appropriate, anchor performances and moderation make subjective ratings more dependable. Scorers should not let neat handwriting or a candidate's identity influence a writing mark unless it belongs to the construct.

Standard setting is different from marking

Marking answers “What score did the student earn?” Standard setting answers “What score counts as sufficient for this decision, and why?” A cut score chosen solely because half the cohort must fail is norm driven. A criterion based process begins by describing a minimally competent performer, then judges what tasks or items that performer should handle and reviews the consequences. In an Angoff type method, judges estimate the chance that a minimally competent candidate would answer each item correctly; estimates are combined and discussed to propose a cut score. For speaking and writing, examine benchmark performances around the boundary. Recheck classification errors and impact before finalising a consequential threshold.

A raw 18/25 has meaning only when linked to task difficulty, scoring, the intended standard and the decision. A percentage correct, a percentile, a band level and a pass classification communicate different things. State which one you report. A criterion referenced report such as “can extract key details from short announcements” may help a learner more than rank alone.

7 Checking items with numbers and with people

Quantitative analysis asks whether an item behaves as expected; qualitative analysis asks why. Use both. A distractor that is never chosen may be implausible; a difficult item may require a skill the course never taught; a surprisingly high score may result from an obvious clue. Do not discard an item merely because it is difficult: the target level and intended decision matter.

Facility or difficulty index

For a dichotomous item, facility p = number correct ÷ number attempting. If 18 of 30 students get it right, p = 18/30 = .60. In this convention, a higher p means an easier item. Some sources call a different quantity “difficulty”; always state the formula and direction. A very easy item can be appropriate for a minimum competency decision. A very hard item may help distinguish advanced learners if the content is appropriate.

Discrimination

Discrimination asks whether higher overall performers tend to answer correctly more often than lower performers. For equal high and low groups, D = p(high group) − p(low group). Suppose the top 10 students answer correctly 8 times and the bottom 10 answer correctly 3 times: D = .8 − .3 = +.5. This item discriminates in the expected direction. If top 10 get 2 right and bottom 10 get 7 right, D = −.5: check an incorrect key, ambiguous wording, teaching mismatch or chance. When comparing a single item to total score, it is preferable to use a corrected item total association that excludes that item from the total.

For polytomous writing rubrics, inspect the distribution of scores and rater agreement rather than forcing an essay into a simplistic right/wrong item formula. Reliability coefficients also need the correct context: a high internal consistency estimate does not prove construct coverage, fairness or productive backwash.

Review pilot evidence

In a pilot, ask expert reviewers whether tasks represent the objectives, are linguistically clear and treat groups fairly. Observe timing and administration. Ask a few learners what they thought each instruction meant; this can reveal a response process different from the intended one. Inspect omitted answers, distractor choices, rater disagreements and comments. Revise and pilot again where the stakes warrant it. Fulcher and Davidson (2007, units on items, scoring and test production) connect such procedures to an explicit argument about what scores mean.

8 What a communicative or task based test measures

Older tests often treated language as a stock of separate forms. Yet someone who can select the correct past tense in isolation may struggle to ask for help, understand an announcement or adjust an explanation to a listener. Communicative language testing begins with the ability to make and understand meaning in a social situation. The question changes from “Does the student recognise the right form?” to “Can the student use language effectively and appropriately for this purpose?” This does not make grammar irrelevant: form helps convey meaning, but is considered alongside discourse, purpose and interaction.

Communicative competence is commonly associated with Hymes's challenge to a purely grammatical account of competence, and with Canale and Swain's account of components of communicative competence. In an exam answer, apply the idea rather than merely list names: a student who gives grammatically accurate but rude or irrelevant replies may have language form without adequate sociolinguistic or discourse appropriateness. Bachman and Palmer (1996) analyse language ability in relation to language use and task characteristics; their framework helps a designer ask what a real task demands from a particular learner in a particular situation.

Bachman and Palmer’s usefulness framework: They ask designers to balance reliability, construct validity, authenticity, interactiveness, impact and practicality for a particular testing situation. Authenticity concerns the relationship between test tasks and target language use; interactiveness concerns how a task engages the test taker’s language ability and other relevant characteristics; impact concerns effects on people and institutions. No single quality has an identical priority in every context. This is a useful theoretical extension of the four practical qualities discussed above, and Fulcher and Davidson (2007, Unit A1) explicitly discuss the framework.

Authenticity has at least two relevant senses. A task may resemble the features of a target situation, such as reading an actual notice before requesting information. It may also require the learner to engage in the kinds of language processes the intended use demands. A real newspaper extract pasted into a test is not automatically a sound task if the questions require only locating copied words. Conversely, a carefully adapted classroom notice may produce relevant evidence for school aged learners.

Task based language assessment places a meaningful task at the centre of evidence gathering. Students might compare two schedules and agree on a plan, write a reply to an enquiry, or follow spoken directions to identify a destination. A task has an input, a purpose, expected conditions and an observable outcome. In design, identify the target domain, choose representative task features, constrain background knowledge, decide whether success and language quality are both scored, and make individual contributions visible. Task completion alone can be misleading: a student might finish by pointing or by having a stronger partner do all the talking. A rubric should reflect the claim about language ability.

Three tasks compared

“Choose the correct preposition” is discrete point grammar recognition. “Describe your last journey for two minutes” directly elicits extended speaking, though the listener's role may be limited. “Ask a partner about two travel choices, resolve a timetable conflict and agree on a plan” elicits interaction and a meaningful outcome. Choose among them by the ability you intend to infer, not by assuming that the last format is always superior.

9 How language testing arrived here

Historical frameworks help explain why exam designs differ. An essay translation tradition favoured extended writing, translation, grammar explanation and teacher judgment. It could elicit complex language but had inconsistent sampling and scoring. A structuralist or psychometric approach favoured separate items and standardised scoring, often making grammar or vocabulary easier to count. Its strength was broad, consistent sampling of discrete knowledge; its weakness appeared when scores on isolated forms were interpreted as full communicative ability.

An integrative reaction used tasks such as cloze and dictation that combine several linguistic resources. Integration still need not represent the social purpose of using language. Communicative approaches then prioritised meaningful language use, roles, audiences and contexts; performance and task based assessment extended this concern to authentic acts and outcomes. Current work also considers fairness, consequences, technology and multiple sources of evidence. These are tendencies, not sealed eras: a modern test can use both well written MCQs for reading and a direct oral interview for speaking. The intelligent question is what each method can support.

10 Fairness bias ethics and linguistic diversity

A fair test gives candidates a reasonable chance to show the target ability and uses the result only for a defensible purpose. Fairness is not achieved merely by giving everyone identical paper. If a test of listening uses a defective speaker in one room, formal sameness masks unequal opportunity. If a speaking prompt assumes costly foreign travel, background experience becomes an unintended influence. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 4) connect validity with fair opportunity, suitable accommodations, good administration and appropriate score use.

Bias occurs when an item, process or interpretation systematically advantages or disadvantages a group for reasons unrelated to the ability being measured. Examine content, accent familiarity, inaccessible visual or audio presentation, gendered assumptions, socio economic experience and language variety. Do not equate cultural difference with lower competence. Expert review, diverse pilot groups, candidate feedback and comparative item analysis can reveal risks; any statistical difference needs thoughtful interpretation rather than automatic deletion.

Accommodation modifies access conditions while preserving the construct: for instance, an accessible interface for a visually impaired candidate on a grammar test. The same modification might alter what a listening test measures, so specify the intended ability and the reason for the accommodation. Inclusive design offers readable instructions, accessible layouts, explainable procedures and equivalent opportunities from the outset. For multilingual candidates, distinguish English ability from irrelevant unfamiliarity with a locally specific cultural reference.

Ethics: tell candidates the purpose, procedure, scoring and use of results; protect responses and recorded speech; restrict access to data; check test security without humiliating candidates; provide a fair appeal or correction route for consequential decisions; and avoid treating a noisy one day score as an infallible description of a person. High stakes examinations can create anxiety, coaching and a narrowed curriculum, while also enabling access if their standards and procedures are defensible. Evaluate consequences for students and teachers, especially those with fewer resources. McNamara and Roever's social perspective, listed on the syllabus, can be cited for the broader point that tests function within institutions and social inequalities; do not pretend an unprovided text establishes a specific page level claim.

11 Assessment that helps teaching while learning happens

Formative assessment uses evidence to adjust learning and teaching while there is still time to improve. A teacher listens to pairs explaining a chart, notices that they can describe figures but cannot compare them, models comparative language and asks them to try again. The crucial element is the use of evidence; a midterm is not automatically formative just because it occurs midcourse. A final mark used only to rank students is generally summative. The same quiz can have formative and summative uses if it contributes to a grade and informs a useful next step.

Self assessment asks learners to judge their work against known criteria. A student checks whether her email states its purpose, provides reasons and uses an appropriate closing. Peer assessment asks classmates to give criterion based feedback, for example identifying one supported claim and one unclear transition. These practices can develop awareness and autonomy, but untrained praise or harsh comments are unreliable and unhelpful. Model the criteria, show an example, limit the feedback task and retain teacher oversight for consequential marks.

Dynamic assessment examines learning potential through supported performance: assess an initial attempt, provide a prompt or mediation, observe how the learner responds, then consider what can be done with help and independently. Imagine a student cannot infer a word's meaning; after the teacher asks her to examine the surrounding sentence, she succeeds. The response to mediation is informative beyond a static right/wrong result. It is especially useful diagnostically, although standardising the assistance and fitting it into a large high stakes examination can be difficult. The theoretical connection is commonly made to Vygotsky's zone of proximal development; the name earns credit only when you explain how mediation reveals emerging ability.

Portfolios and classroom observation gather performances over time and can show revision, range and growth. Select evidence and criteria deliberately; otherwise a large folder becomes a collection, not an assessment. Combining such evidence with tests can help a teacher make better instructional decisions, but a school must decide how it will moderate and document judgments when grades have consequences.

12 Computer based adaptive and online assessment

Computer based testing delivers material and collects responses through a device. It can automate secure distribution, timing, item randomisation, immediate scoring for selected responses and data analysis. It can also present audio and video more consistently than poor classroom playback. Its challenges include unequal access, typing familiarity, accessibility, connectivity, screen size, privacy, security and equivalence between modes. Moving a paper test onto a screen can change difficulty; pilot and compare performance before assuming the same score interpretation holds.

Computer adaptive testing selects items as the system updates an ability estimate. A first medium item may be followed by a harder one after a strong response; a candidate's final estimate is based on item characteristics and the pattern of responses, not simply the count correct. Efficient measurement depends on a calibrated bank, content balancing, exposure control and a clear stopping rule. A system that merely sends a learner to an easier page after a wrong answer is branching practice, not necessarily a properly validated adaptive test.

Online platforms can help with frequent low stakes quizzes, remote practice and feedback. They also introduce device inequity, authentication difficulties, cheating opportunities and service failure. Remote oral assessment may need a backup procedure. Automatic speech or writing scoring can support quick feedback but requires validation for the intended learners and varieties of English; a fluent sounding model score is not evidence that its criteria are fair. For Bangladesh, a practical design should account for intermittent electricity or internet and unequal access to quiet rooms or laptops. Always have a plan for interrupted sessions that treats students consistently.

13 How to evaluate an unseen test in the examination

The uploaded question sheet says “Evaluate the enclosed test,” but contains no enclosed test. Here is a procedure you can apply to whatever the examiner supplies. Begin by identifying the test's apparent learners, purpose and score use from the cover, instructions and tasks. If these are absent, explicitly say that the missing information limits evaluation. Then inspect the paper in six passes.

  1. Construct and coverage: Which skills are claimed? Which are actually sampled? Compare with course objectives or reasonable target uses. Do not fault a grammar quiz for lacking oral tasks if its purpose truly is checking grammar.
  2. Task quality: Are prompts, passages and items level appropriate? Is each key defensible? Does an item test comprehension or merely copied wording or unrelated subject knowledge?
  3. Scoring: Are marks, acceptable answers and performance descriptors clear? Could two markers agree? Is the weighting appropriate to the intended abilities?
  4. Practicality and administration: Is time enough? Are equipment and staffing plausible? Do instructions specify what students and raters should do?
  5. Fairness and consequences: Are any cultural or accessibility barriers irrelevant to the target ability? How might the paper shape classroom teaching?
  6. Repair: For each major weakness propose one concrete revision and say what evidence you would collect in a pilot.
Reusable answer paragraph

“The paper appears to be a final achievement test of secondary English. Its reading passage and related questions provide some direct evidence of reading comprehension, but the short grammar items cannot by themselves support a claim about spoken communication. The 15 mark composition offers writing evidence; however, the prompt should state its audience and purpose, and the marking scheme needs descriptors for task fulfilment, organisation and language. A brief sampled listening task and a paired oral task would improve coverage if those abilities occur in the stated learning outcomes. Before operational use I would pilot the questions for timing and ambiguity and moderate a sample of scripts.”

When the actual enclosure is available, replace every hypothetical statement with observed details. Quote its item numbers or mark allocations where possible. An evaluation without specific evidence from the enclosed paper will read like a memorised essay.

Question 1 Teaching testing and harmful backwash

Teaching establishes intended learning outcomes and gives learners opportunities to reach them; testing gathers evidence about attainment and informs decisions. Therefore the intended ability and teaching goals should guide test design. Test results then feed back into teaching by revealing what needs further work. The influence of a test on instruction is called backwash or washback.

Consider a course whose goal includes participating in spoken discussion. If its examination rewards only memorised grammatical answers, students rationally practise what earns marks, and teachers may devote less time to interaction. The test has encouraged harmful backwash because its tasks underrepresent an important objective. If the test includes purposeful, fairly scored discussion and students consequently practise explaining and responding to ideas, beneficial backwash becomes possible. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 6) connect task choice and clear specifications with efforts to encourage positive backwash.

To limit harm, start with explicit course objectives, write specifications that represent them, assess valued skills directly where feasible, publish sample tasks and scoring criteria, and avoid giving an overwhelming share of marks to easily scored but marginal content. Pilot tasks, train raters, and use results to diagnose which teaching approaches require adjustment. These measures also require practical support: an oral component with no trained raters or feasible timetable may produce inconsistent marks. Teaching should determine what is worth assessing, while assessment evidence helps teachers improve instruction; neither relationship is a one way command.

Question 2 Purposes of tests

Language tests are designed for decisions, and the decision determines the required evidence. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 3) distinguish four principal purposes. A proficiency test asks whether a candidate can use a language to a specified level or for a target situation, independently of a particular course; for example, whether an applicant can study in English. An achievement test checks attainment of a course's objectives. A progress achievement test occurs during a course; a final achievement test at its conclusion.

A diagnostic test identifies specific strengths and weaknesses so the teacher can plan suitable instruction. If a learner's overall score is 55%, a diagnostic profile might show good gist reading but weak listening for detail. A placement test puts a new learner into an appropriate level or group; its most important outcome is the placement decision, not a detailed causal explanation of errors.

One instrument may supply information for more than one purpose, but its tasks, score reports and supporting evidence must suit each proposed decision. A general proficiency score does not automatically indicate which unit of a local course the learner has mastered. Similarly, a short classroom achievement quiz cannot by itself justify a high stakes university admission decision. Purpose is therefore the starting point for both test construction and evaluation.

Question 3 Criteria for a successful test

A successful test yields useful evidence for a stated decision. Hughes and Hughes (2020, chs. 2, 4–6) place validity, reliability, practicality and positive backwash at the centre of design. Validity asks whether the tasks and scores support the intended interpretation: a claim about speaking requires spoken performance, relevant conditions and a rubric attentive to meaning and interaction. Reliability asks whether equivalent performance receives reasonably consistent scores across prompts, raters and administrations. Standard instructions, appropriate task sampling, clear keys and trained raters reduce avoidable error.

Practicality asks whether the test fits available time, cost, staff and equipment. A technically attractive hour long interview for every candidate may be impossible in a large school. Positive backwash asks whether preparation encourages the abilities that teaching values; a direct task can encourage relevant practice, while marks for memorised fragments can narrow the curriculum. Fair access, security and ethical score use must also be considered. Fulcher and Davidson (2007) help locate these qualities within a broader test development and validation process.

The criteria interact. Objective grammar items can increase marking consistency and reduce cost, but they will not replace direct evidence of oral interaction. A short paired oral task with standard prompts, a concise analytic rubric, examiner training and a realistic timetable can improve the balance. The appropriate test is the one whose compromises are justified for its specific purpose.

Question 4 Validity reliability and an HSC English test

Validity concerns whether evidence supports an interpretation of scores for a particular use; reliability concerns the consistency of those scores under comparable conditions. Hughes and Hughes (2020, chs. 4–5) make both central. An examination that consistently scores grammar MCQs could be reliable for grammar recognition but insufficient to claim that students can hold a spoken conversation. Conversely, an oral interview may elicit relevant ability but remain unreliable if raters apply different standards.

To evaluate an HSC English paper, I would first compare its stated curriculum outcomes and score uses with its actual tasks and marks. If a given version primarily assesses written reading, grammar and composition while its intended claims include listening and speaking, the latter are underrepresented. If writing questions permit rehearsed texts, they may provide weak evidence of flexible writing. Reading tasks should require comprehension of a suitable passage rather than general knowledge or word matching.

Reliability requires unambiguous instructions and answer keys for selected responses; written production requires common rubrics, exemplars and some double marking or moderation. Comparable time, printing and room conditions matter too. A feasible improvement would be a published blueprint, improved writing prompts and marking guides, and phased listening and oral tasks with teacher preparation and quality checks. I would examine actual contemporary papers and curriculum documents before asserting that any particular weakness applies to the current examination. A useful evaluation identifies a specific task, the inference it supports, and the evidence missing for a broader claim.

Question 5 Design a writing test for first year English Honours students

Purpose and construct: This final achievement component tests whether first year students can write a coherent, appropriately addressed argument on a familiar academic issue. The construct includes task fulfilment, reasoning, organisation, cohesion and language control. It does not require specialist factual knowledge. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 9) emphasise eliciting representative writing and scoring it according to the intended ability.

Task and conditions: “Your department is considering a weekly student led reading circle. Write a 220–280 word email to the department chair recommending whether to introduce it. Give two reasons, address one likely concern and propose a practical arrangement.” Allow 35 minutes, provide the prompt and length guidance, and forbid prepared material. If the department intends to infer broad writing ability, add a second task in another genre or state that this single task supports a narrower conclusion.

Scoring: Award 10 marks analytically: task fulfilment and audience 4; organisation and cohesion 3; vocabulary and grammar in service of meaning 3. Prepare descriptors and anchor scripts for each band. Train markers with common examples, have a second marker independently rate a sample, review large disagreements and document how off topic or incomplete work is treated. Avoid letting handwriting dominate. The task directly elicits writing, the analytic procedure makes scores explainable, and 35 minute timing and a concise rubric make administration feasible. Pilot with similar students to check topic accessibility, writing time, score spread and marker agreement.

Question 6 Principles for an oral test in a Bangladeshi primary school

An oral test for primary pupils should ask children to use English in short, familiar situations at an age appropriate level. Begin with a clear purpose: for instance, whether pupils can name familiar objects, convey simple information and ask and answer basic questions. A picture supported warm up, a short description of a familiar scene and an information gap with an examiner or peer can sample more than memorised recital. Instructions should be demonstrated in language children understand; unclear instructions should not be mistaken for poor speaking.

Assess whether the child communicates the intended message, responds to a question, uses intelligible speech and attempts appropriate simple words and structures. An analytic rubric with a few observable bands can be more useful than an undefined “good English” mark. Keep sessions brief, prompts comparable and examiner behaviour supportive but standardised. Pilot the pictures to avoid unfamiliar cultural content, and make appropriate accommodations for disabilities while preserving the purpose of the test. Record or double score a small sample where feasible to monitor consistency.

Practicality is particularly important when classes are large. Testing pairs on a rota while other pupils do supervised work may reduce time, but each child's contribution must be scored separately. Feedback such as “can answer familiar questions; needs practice asking for information” connects testing to teaching. Hughes and Hughes (2020, chs. 10 and 15) provide the relevant practical grounding in speaking tests and tests for young learners.

Question 7 Design listening and speaking for higher secondary students

The proposed assessment has two 10 mark parts. In listening, learners hear a 90 second dialogue about a college club timetable twice and answer five short questions covering the main decision and relevant details. Each response is worth two marks; the key lists acceptable equivalent wording. Students preview the questions briefly, and the recording, audibility and timing are standardised. In speaking, pairs receive two partly different timetable cards and have four minutes to decide which event to attend, exchange missing information and justify their decision. Each student is scored separately for task fulfilment and interaction 4, fluency and intelligibility 3, language range and control 3.

The listening task directly elicits comprehension of spoken information; the speaking task elicits meaningful exchange. Task specifications, comparable prompt sets and a review of whether target situations are represented protect content validity. Pilot the audio for clarity and the paired task for equal opportunity to speak. Train raters using sample performances, use concise descriptors and moderate a sample of scores to support reliability. Schedule pairs across classes, use available audio equipment and prepare a backup for equipment failure to support practicality. Publish the task format and criteria beforehand so candidates can prepare through useful listening and speaking practice.

Short notes in a compact five mark format

Backwash: The influence of testing on teaching and learning. A grammar only high stakes test can narrow lessons to drills even when the curriculum values interaction: harmful backwash. A well specified direct oral component can encourage relevant speaking practice: potentially beneficial backwash. The effect depends on stakes and teaching conditions. Hughes and Hughes (2020, ch. 6) advocate representative tasks and transparent criteria.

Reliability coefficient: A numerical estimate of score consistency obtained through an appropriate procedure such as parallel forms, internal consistency or test–retest. Values closer to 1 generally imply less measurement error in that context, but do not show that the intended skill is being measured. Explain the method, population and score use before interpreting the value. Rater agreement is a separate concern for performance tasks.

Holistic and analytic scoring: Holistic marking makes one overall judgment about a performance. Analytic marking rates dimensions such as task fulfilment, organisation and language separately, then combines scores. The first is often quicker; the second helps feedback and clarifies criteria. Both need descriptors, exemplars and training to limit inconsistency.

Subjective versus objective scoring: With an objective key, the scorer need not judge the quality of an answer, as in a well keyed MCQ. With subjective scoring, a rater judges the quality of an essay or speech. The distinction concerns scoring discretion, not whether a test is valuable. Rubrics and moderation make subjective scoring more consistent.

Practicality: The feasibility of designing, giving, marking and reporting a test with available time, staff, facilities and money. A five minute interview for every candidate may be difficult in a large school; paired tasks can reduce staffing demands. Practicality is judged together with validity and reliability: speed alone cannot justify excluding the ability the test claims to measure.

Norm and criterion referenced: A norm referenced interpretation locates performance relative to others, such as a percentile. A criterion referenced interpretation relates performance to a defined ability standard, such as being able to write a clear formal request. They answer different questions; “75% correct” alone supplies neither a comparative rank nor a fully described performance standard.

Retrieval questions

1 A test has clear marking but measures only grammar while claiming speaking ability. What is the primary problem?

Validity: the tasks underrepresent speaking. Clear marking can improve reliability without supplying the missing spoken evidence.

2 A learner receives 62 on Monday and 82 on Tuesday with no plausible learning. What should you investigate?

Reliability: task sampling, administration conditions, scoring, temporary learner conditions and whether the forms were equivalent.

3 A reading task asks for three essay length answers. What hidden influence may distort the reading score?

Writing skill may contribute construct irrelevant variance; use shorter responses or score reading evidence separately if the intended construct is reading.

4 The top 10 students get an item correct 3 times and the bottom 10 get it correct 7 times. What is D?

D = .3 − .7 = −.4. Check key, ambiguity, curriculum match and other causes before revising or discarding.

5 A teacher returns a quiz with feedback and reteaches weak areas. Is it formative?

Yes, to the extent the information is actually used to guide improvement while learning continues.

6 A percentile rank of 80 is reported. Has the candidate answered 80% of questions correctly?

Not necessarily. The percentile describes relative position in a reference group, not proportion correct.

7 A speaking prompt is authentic but a partner speaks for almost the whole task. What has gone wrong?

The performance yields insufficient individual evidence. Standardise turn opportunities and score each learner's contribution separately.
